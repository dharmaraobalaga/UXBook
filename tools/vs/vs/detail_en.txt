Complete explanation – Telugu first + English terminology.

Let's understand these four roles in detail using a single digital product example.

First, an important point:

Product Manager (PM), Product Designer (PD), UX Designer and UI Designer are four different job titles. But while a product is being built, their responsibilities are connected to one another.

In some companies a single Product Designer handles both UX and UI. Larger companies have separate designers for UX, UI, Research and Product Strategy.

Our example: imagine we are building a food delivery app like Swiggy.

A customer should be able to find the food they want easily, order it, pay and track the delivery. PM, PD, UX and UI work together to create this whole experience.

### 1 · Product Manager (PM)
Product Manager – What should we build? Why should we build it?

1.1 Who is a Product Manager?:

A Product Manager is the person who understands a digital product's business goals, user needs, market opportunities and technical feasibility, and decides the direction in which the product should develop.

Put simply:

Product Manager = What to build + Why to build + What to prioritise

In other words:
- Which product should we make?
- Why should we make it?
- Who should it be useful for?
- Which features should be developed first?
- What benefit should it bring to the business?
- How should the product's success be measured?

Finding the answers to these questions is the PM's main responsibility.

1.2 Key responsibilities of a Product Manager:

1. Product Vision

Sets a clear direction for what the product should look like in the future and which problem it should solve.

Example:

Food Delivery App Vision:

"Customers should be able to discover, order and receive food conveniently and reliably."

2. Business Goals

Decides the results the company wants to achieve through the product.

Examples:
- Increase revenue.
- Acquire new customers.
- Get existing customers to order again.
- Reduce order cancellations.
- Increase customer retention.

3. Market Research

Studies other products in the market, competitors, trends and customer needs.

Example – in apps like Swiggy and Zomato:
- Which features are working well?
- Which problems are customers facing?
- What new opportunities are there in our product?

4. User Needs & Problem Identification

Understands the problems users are facing.

Example:

"Customers are abandoning their food orders during checkout."

That is, customers leave at the checkout stage without completing the order.

5. Product Roadmap

Creates the plan for which feature should be developed when.

6. Prioritisation

We can't build every feature at once, so the PM decides what matters most and what should be done first.

Example:

Feature	Priority
Simplified Checkout	High
Multiple Payment Options	High
Dark Mode	Low
Animated Food Icons	Low

7. Requirement Definition

Defines the requirements for how each feature should work.

Documents used for this:
- PRD – Product Requirements Document
- Product Roadmap
- User Stories
- Acceptance Criteria
- Feature Specifications

8. Stakeholder Management

Coordinates with teams such as Business, Design, Engineering, Marketing, Sales and Operations.

9. Product Success Measurement

After the product is released, checks with data whether it has been successful.

Example metrics:
- Conversion Rate
- Customer Retention
- Monthly Active Users (MAU)
- Daily Active Users (DAU)
- Revenue
- Churn Rate

Tool	Use
Jira	Tasks & sprint tracking
Confluence	Documentation

Aha!	Product roadmap
Google Analytics	Product analytics
Mixpanel	User behaviour analytics
Amplitude	Product usage analysis
Excel / Google Sheets	Data analysis

1.4 Key deliverables of a PM:

- Product Vision
- Product Strategy
- Product Roadmap
- PRD
- Product Backlog
- User Stories
- Acceptance Criteria
- Release Plan
- Product Metrics Dashboard

1.5 Food Delivery App – PM Example:

Suppose data shows that 40% of customers leave before payment while ordering food.

What does the Product Manager do?

1. Identifies the problem.
2. Examines the analytics.
3. Collects customer feedback.
4. Makes the checkout problem a priority.
5. Discusses it with the design and development teams.
6. Sets a target for the improvement.
7. Measures the results after the feature is released.

The PM focuses mainly on product success, business goals and priorities.

### 2 · Product Designer (PD)
Product Designer – How do we give a complete design solution to the problem?

2.1 Who is a Product Designer?:

A Product Designer is the person who understands user needs, business goals and technical constraints, and creates the complete design experience for a product.

They don't just design screens. They understand the user's problem, think of the right solution, design it, test it and improve it.

Put simply:

Product Designer = Problem Solving + UX + UI + Product Thinking

In other words: they think about everything together – what the user's problem is, how to solve it, whether the solution will be useful to the user, whether it benefits the business, and whether it is technically possible.

2.2 Key responsibilities of a Product Designer:

1. Problem Understanding

Understands the problem the PM identified in more depth.

Example: customers are leaving during checkout.

Things the Product Designer examines:
- Is the checkout process too long?
- Are the payment options unclear?
- Do the delivery charges appear only at the end?
- Is address selection complicated?
- Is the user not feeling confident (trust)?

2. User Research

Does research – or works with a UX Researcher – to understand users' behaviour, needs and problems.

Methods:
- User Interviews
- Surveys
- Contextual Inquiry
- Competitor Analysis
- Behaviour Analysis
- Usability Testing

3. Design Strategy

Creates the strategy for what kind of design solution to give the problem.

Example:

"Let's simplify the checkout experience by reducing unnecessary steps and improving price transparency."

4. Information Architecture (IA)

Decides how information should be arranged in the product, which screen goes where, and what the user should see first.

5. User Journey & User Flows

Designs the experience of how the user travels from start to finish.

6. Wireframing

Creates the basic structure of the product's screens.

7. Interaction Design

Designs buttons, menus, forms, navigation, and how the system should respond to user actions.

8. Prototyping

Builds the design in an interactive form and tests the user experience.

9. UI Design

Creates the product's visual design, typography, colours, layouts and components.

10. Design System

Creates components, tokens, typography and colours so that the same design rules apply across the product.

11. Usability Testing

Tests whether users can complete their tasks easily using the design.

12. Developer Collaboration

Works with developers so the design is implemented correctly.

13. Post-launch Improvement

After release, improves the design based on user feedback and analytics.

2.3 Deliverables of a Product Designer:

Stage	Deliverables
Research	Research insights, personas
Strategy	Problem statement, design principles
Structure	Information architecture, sitemap
Experience	User journey, user flow
Design	Wireframes, interaction design
Validation	Prototype, usability findings
Visual	High-fidelity UI, design system
Handoff	Design specifications, annotations
Improvement	Design iterations, optimisation

2.4 Tools a Product Designer uses:

- Figma – UI, UX, prototyping
- FigJam – Workshops, user flows
- Miro – Research collaboration
- Maze – Usability testing
- Dovetail – Research analysis
- Jira – Development collaboration
- Mixpanel / Amplitude – Product behaviour analysis

2.5 Food Delivery App – Product Designer Example:

To solve the checkout problem, the Product Designer thinks like this.

Proposed solution:

1. Make address selection easier.
2. Show the order summary clearly.
3. Show delivery charges up front.
4. Keep payment options easily available.
5. Reduce the checkout steps.
6. Show the order confirmation clearly.

This solution is created as a wireframe and prototype and then tested.

The Product Designer balances user needs, business goals and technical feasibility.

### 3 · UX Designer (UX)
User Experience Designer – How easy should it be for the user to use the product?

3.1 Who is a UX Designer?:

UX means User Experience.

The whole experience a user has while using a website or mobile app is called the User Experience.

The UX Designer's main responsibility is to understand users' needs, behaviour and problems, and to design so the user can complete their task easily, efficiently and without confusion.

Put simply:

UX Designer = User Research + User Understanding + Usability + Interaction Design

In other words:
- Who is the user?
- What do they want?
- What problem are they facing?
- What do they want to do in the app?
- How many steps does it take to complete that task?
- Where do they get confused?
- How can that experience be improved?

Finding the answers to these questions is the UX Designer's responsibility.

3.2 Key responsibilities of a UX Designer:

1. User Research

Understanding users' needs, behaviour, problems and expectations.

Research methods:

Method	Use
User Interviews	Learning users' opinions
Surveys	Collecting opinions from many users
Observation	Watching how users work
Contextual Inquiry	Observing the user while they work in their real environment
Competitor Analysis	Analysing competing products
Usability Testing	Testing whether the design is easy to use

Example:

In a food delivery app, customers are struggling to place orders. The UX Designer interviews some customers and observes their behaviour.

Problems like these may come out:
- Delivery charges are not shown up front.
- The payment process is hard to understand.
- Changing the address is difficult.
- The order summary is not clear.

2. User Persona

Based on research, creates a representative model of the main user types.

Example – Persona: Rahul
- Age: 28
- Profession: Software Engineer
- Goal: Order food quickly.
- Need: Simple checkout and fast delivery.
- Frustration: Too many steps, hidden charges.

A persona does not show a real person's details as they are. It is a model that represents the user characteristics seen in research.

3. Empathy Mapping

Used to understand what the user is thinking, experiencing, saying and doing.

- Says – What is the user saying?
- Thinks – What are they thinking?
- Does – What are they doing?
- Feels – How are they feeling?

4. User Journey Mapping

Maps the user's whole journey until they reach a goal.

Food delivery example:

@@FIG2

Stage	User Action	Possible Pain Point
Discover	App Open	Searching for a restaurant
Explore	Menu Browse	Too many choices
Select	Food Selection	Comparing prices
Checkout	Address & Payment	Payment confusion
Track	Order Tracking	Not knowing the delivery status
Receive	Food Delivery	Delay

The UX Designer identifies opportunities to improve the user experience at every stage.

5. Information Architecture (IA)

Arranging the information, content and features of the app in the right order.

Example – food delivery app structure:
- Home
- Search
- Restaurants
- Categories
- Restaurant Details
- Menu
- Cart
- Checkout
- Payment
- Order Tracking
- Profile
- Order History

This helps the user find the information they need easily.

6. User Flow

Shows the steps a user follows to complete a specific goal.

Example:

Open App → Select Restaurant → Choose Food → Add to Cart → Checkout → Select Address → Payment → Order Confirmation.

7. Task Flow

Close to a User Flow, but usually focuses on the actions needed to complete one specific task.

Example – Task: Add one item to cart.
- Open restaurant.
- Select food item.
- Choose quantity.
- Tap Add to Cart.
- Confirm item in cart.

8. Wireframing

Creates the structure of where each element goes on a screen, where each button sits, and what information appears first.

Wireframes usually prioritise screen structure, content and functionality over colours and visual details.

9. Interaction Design

Designs how the system should respond when the user taps a button, makes a mistake in a form, or while data is loading.

Examples:
- Button click response
- Loading indicator
- Error messages
- Success messages
- Empty states
- Form validation
- Navigation behaviour

10. Prototyping

Connects screens in tools like Figma to make a prototype the user can use like a real app.

11. Usability Testing

The user is given a task and asked to complete it.

Example:

"Please order one vegetarian meal and complete the checkout process."

Things observed:
- Did they complete the task?
- How long did it take?
- Where did they struggle?
- Where did they make mistakes?
- Did they need help?

12. Accessibility

Designing so that users with different needs – age, physical ability, vision, hearing – can also use the product.

Examples:
- Readable text
- Colour contrast
- Screen reader support
- Keyboard accessibility
- Clear labels
- Touch target sizes

3.3 UX Designer Deliverables:

Category	Deliverable
Research	Research Plan, Interview Findings
Understanding	Personas, Empathy Map
Journey	User Journey Map
Structure	Sitemap, Information Architecture
Flow	User Flow, Task Flow
Design	Wireframes, Interaction Design
Validation	Prototype, Usability Test Report
Improvement	UX Recommendations

3.4 Tools a UX Designer uses:

- Figma – Wireframes and prototypes
- FigJam – User flows and workshops
- Miro – Journey mapping
- Maze – Usability testing
- Dovetail – User research analysis
- Optimal Workshop – Information architecture testing
- Google Forms – Surveys
- Hotjar – Behaviour analysis

3.5 UX Designer's Food Delivery Example:

UX problem: customers get confused during checkout.

UX Designer's solution:

1. Conduct user interviews.
2. Analyse the checkout journey.
3. Identify pain points.
4. Improve the user flow.
5. Create wireframes.
6. Build a prototype.
7. Run usability testing.
8. Change the design based on the test results.

The UX Designer focuses mainly on user experience, ease of use and usability.

### 4 · UI Designer (UI)
User Interface Designer – How should the product look?

4.1 Who is a UI Designer?:

UI means User Interface.

The visual part through which a user interacts with a digital product is called the User Interface.

For example:
- Buttons
- Icons
- Typography
- Colours
- Images
- Cards
- Navigation bars
- Forms
- Layouts

The UI Designer creates these elements effectively, beautifully, clearly and in a consistent design style.

Put simply:

UI Designer = Visual Design + Layout + Typography + Colour + Components

In other words: the UI Designer decides how the app or website should look, which colours to use, which font to use, how buttons should look, and how much priority each element gets on the screen.

4.2 Key responsibilities of a UI Designer:

1. Visual Hierarchy

Designing what should be seen first on the screen and what is most important.

Example – food delivery checkout screen:
- Total Amount – should stand out.
- Place Order – a clear CTA button.
- Delivery Address – should be easy to read.
- Terms & Conditions – can be shown with lower priority.

2. Colour System

Chooses suitable colours for the product and gives each a specific use.

Example:

Colour Type	Usage
Primary	Main brand colour
Secondary	Supporting colour
Success	Successful action
Error	Mistake or problem
Warning	Warning
Background	Screen background
Text	Text colours

3. Typography

Decides font, size, weight, line height and letter spacing.

Example:
- Heading – 24 px, Semibold
- Subheading – 18 px, Medium
- Body – 16 px, Regular
- Caption – 12 px, Regular

These are only examples; real values depend on the product's design system.

4. Layout Design

Designs how content, buttons, images and cards are arranged on the screen.

5. Spacing & Grid System

Using the right spacing, alignment and grids in the design. Example:
- 4-point spacing system
- 8-point spacing system
- Consistent margins
- Responsive grids

6. Iconography

Designs the icons used in the app so they share one consistent style.

Example:
- Home
- Search
- Cart
- Profile
- Location
- Notification

7. Components

Creates frequently used UI elements as reusable components.

Examples:
- Buttons
- Input Fields
- Dropdowns
- Cards
- Modals
- Tabs
- Navigation bars
- Checkboxes

8. Design System

Creates rules, components and tokens so the design is consistent across the product.

A design system usually includes:
- Colour Tokens
- Typography Tokens
- Spacing Tokens
- Grid System
- Components
- Component Variants
- Interaction States
- Design Guidelines

9. Responsive Design

Designing so the UI looks right on different screen sizes such as mobile, tablet and desktop.

10. UI States

How should a button or component look in different situations?

For example, button states:
- Default
- Hover
- Focus
- Pressed
- Disabled
- Loading

Form states:
- Empty
- Filled
- Error
- Success
- Disabled

11. Visual Consistency

Making sure every screen uses the same colours, fonts, buttons, spacing and components.

12. Developer Handoff

Provides the details developers need to implement the UI correctly.

- Figma Inspect / Dev Mode
- Component specifications
- Spacing values
- Typography details
- Asset exports
- Interaction states
- Responsive behaviour

Suppose the UX Designer has handed over a checkout wireframe.

The UI Designer turns it into a complete visual design.

Things the UI Designer decides:

1. Brand colours.
2. Typography.

### 5 · PD vs UX vs UI – the real difference
Product Designer vs UX Designer vs UI Designer – what is the real difference?

Understanding the difference between these three roles is very important, because this question is asked often in job interviews.

Aspect	Product Designer	UX Designer	UI Designer
Main focus	Complete product experience	User experience	Visual interface
Problem Solving	End-to-end	User problems	Visual and interface problems
User Research	Does / collaborates	One of the main responsibilities	Collaborates as needed
User Journey	Creates	Main responsibility	Usually not a main responsibility
User Flow	Creates	Main responsibility	Usually not a main responsibility
Wireframes	Creates	Creates	As needed
Interaction Design	Does	Does	Also designs UI interactions
Visual Design	Does	As needed	Main responsibility
Design System	Creates / maintains	Collaborates	Mainly contributes
Usability Testing	Does	One of the main responsibilities	Collaborates on visual issues
Business Goals	Takes into account	Takes into account	Takes into account
Technical Feasibility	Takes into account	Collaborates	Collaborates

Remember: this is only a general comparison of responsibilities. Responsibilities can change with company size, team structure and the job description.

### 6 · Four roles in one project
How do the four roles work in a single project?

Let's continue with our food delivery app example.

Problem Statement:

Customers are abandoning their food orders during checkout.

That is, while ordering food, customers leave at the checkout stage without completing the order.

Step	Role	Responsibility
1	PM	Identify the problem and set the priority
2	PM + PD	Discuss the problem, goals and requirements
3	PD + UX	User research, understanding pain points
4	UX	Create the user flow and wireframes
5	PD + UI	Create the complete visual design
6	PD + UX	Prototype, usability testing
7	UI + Developers	UI implementation, design handoff
8	PM + PD + Team	Analyse results after release

In this process the PM works with the design and development teams at every stage. It is not just an orderly handoff; it is continuous collaboration.

### 7 · Classic Rummy example
An important example – Classic Rummy

Taking your Openplay Technologies experience, you can understand the difference between these four roles like this.

Scenario: new users who enter the Rummy game struggle to understand how to play.

Role	What they would do
Product Manager	Sets improving new-user activation as a product goal.
Product Designer	Designs a solution that fully improves the new user's first experience.
UX Designer	Improves user flows such as onboarding, joining a game and understanding the rules.
UI Designer	Improves cards, buttons, the game table, instructions, typography and visual hierarchy.

Here, in your Product Design role, it is not just about making the game screen look good; you have to think about the whole experience until the user understands the game and plays it.

### 11 · English Summary
A simple English summary to say in an interview

A Product Manager focuses on product strategy, business goals, roadmap and feature prioritisation.

A Product Designer focuses on solving product problems through end-to-end design, balancing user needs, business objectives and technical feasibility.

A UX Designer focuses on understanding users and creating easy, meaningful and usable experiences through research, user journeys, information architecture and interaction design.

A UI Designer focuses on the visual interface, including colours, typography, layouts, icons, components and design systems.

All four roles collaborate to create a successful digital product.
